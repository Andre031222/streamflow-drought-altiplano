\abstract*{Short-range streamflow forecasts and early drought signals are valuable in the tropical Andes, but published machine-learning gains are hard to compare because evaluation protocols are rarely leakage-free. We release an open benchmark of \nGauges{} long-record gauges from Andean-GC: \nGaugesAlti{} on the Altiplano, including Puente Isla Cabanillas (Lake Titicaca basin), \nGaugesCB{} in the Cordillera Blanca and \nGaugesWSA{} in the western/southern Andes. \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Streamflow in the tropical Andes sustains irrigation, hydropower and drinking water for millions of people, yet it is shaped by an extreme seasonal cycle, retreating glaciers and recurrent drought \cite{vuille2018andes,baraer2012glacier,lavado2012peru}. On the Altiplano, the endorheic plateau shared by Peru, Bolivia and Chile, rivers such as the Coata--Cabanillas feed Lake Titicaca and depend almost entirely on a short austral-summer wet season \cite{garreaud2003altiplano}; climate projections point to more frequent and longer hydrological droughts in the Titicaca--Desaguadero--Poop\'o system \cite{zubieta2021titicaca}. Short-range discharge forecasts and early drought signals are therefore valuable, but gauges are sparse, records are gappy, and operational tools are rarely open.

Machine learning (ML) is increasingly used for data-driven streamflow forecasting \cite{yaseen2015ai,mosavi2018flood,kratzert2018lstm,kratzert2019lstm,nearing2021role}. For daily lead times, however, discharge is strongly autocorrelated, and the naive persistence forecast $\hat{Q}_{t+h}=Q_t$ is a demanding benchmark \cite{pappenberger2015benchmarks,murphy1988skill}. Reported gains can be inflated by (i) comparing models against weak references, (ii) randomly shuffled cross-validation that leaks temporal information \cite{bergmeir2012cv,roberts2017cv,torres2026blockedcv}, and (iii) implementation details that give baselines and learners different information at issue time. We found the last issue in an earlier version of our own pipeline: the learners saw discharge only up to $t-1$ while persistence used $Q_t$. Calendar gaps were also treated as consecutive days. Correcting both reversed the one-day conclusion, which motivated the protocol reported here.

This paper contributes an open, laptop-scale benchmark for the Peruvian Andes and the Altiplano that makes these choices explicit. Specifically, we:
\begin{enumerate}
\item assemble \nGauges{} long-record gauges from the Andean-GC dataset \cite{aguayo2026andeangc}, covering three regions: \nGaugesAlti{} Altiplano gauges (Cabanillas, Peru, and the Lauca/Caquena headwaters, Chile), \nGaugesCB{} glacier-fed Cordillera Blanca gauges and \nGaugesWSA{} western/southern Andean gauges. We fuse them with ERA5 reanalysis forcing \cite{hersbach2020era5}, MERRA-2-based NASA POWER precipitation and GloFAS~v4 simulated discharge;
\item define a leakage-free protocol: a calendar-continuous index, identical information at issue time for all methods, chronological splits with an embargo, hyper-parameter selection on validation only, and expanding-window walk-forward checks;
\item report skill against persistence with moving-block bootstrap intervals \cite{kunsch1989block} and Diebold--Mariano tests \cite{diebold1995dm,harvey1997dm}, at 1-, 3- and 7-day leads;
\item evaluate drought detection directly, using the variable-threshold level method \cite{vanloon2015explained,fleig2006threshold} and categorical scores \cite{schaefer1990csi}, and release a nonparametric standardized streamflow index \cite{farahmand2015nonparametric}.
\end{enumerate}
All code, data extracts and figures are openly available in the project repository: {\small\url{\githubUrl}}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Data-driven streamflow forecasting in the Andes.} Random forests have been used for runoff and flash-flood forecasting in Ecuadorian Andean catchments \cite{munoz2018rf}, recently combined with satellite precipitation and hydrological knowledge \cite{munoz2025rf}. Support-vector regression was tested for monthly runoff in the Central Andes of Argentina \cite{teverovsky2023svr}. In Peru, artificial neural networks have forecast monthly flows in the high-Andean Crisnejas basin \cite{ramos2023crisnejas}, and $k$-nearest neighbours daily flows of the Ramis River on the Altiplano \cite{quispe2023ramis}. Ensemble data assimilation with a conceptual model improved hourly forecasts in the Vilcanota basin \cite{llauca2023vilcanota}. On the Altiplano, Lujano et al.\ found that reanalysis precipitation performs well against gauges, but gridded forcing can still degrade conceptual-model streamflow \cite{lujano2025gridded}. That result motivates both our reanalysis-forced features and the data-fusion ablation. Large-sample LSTM studies set the methodological reference for data-driven hydrology \cite{kratzert2018lstm,kratzert2019lstm,gauch2021lstm,addor2017camels,nearing2021role}. Most Andean studies, however, evaluate one basin, one lead time, and NSE without a persistence reference or uncertainty. Our prior multi-station climate-ML study of Altiplano frost \cite{torres2025frost} used the same comparative-benchmark design.

\textbf{Validation under dependence.} Random $k$-fold cross-validation is optimistic for autocorrelated or spatially structured data \cite{bergmeir2012cv,roberts2017cv}. For Peruvian agricultural survey data, blocked cross-validation revealed that random folds overstated accuracy \cite{torres2026blockedcv}, and hierarchical spatial models were needed to characterise climate vulnerability \cite{torres2026bayescar}. We transfer the same principle to time: chronological blocks with an embargo, and walk-forward refits.

\textbf{Hydrological drought.} Hydrological drought is a below-normal availability of streamflow or storage \cite{vanloon2015explained,mishra2010review}. It is identified with threshold-level methods \cite{fleig2006threshold,vanloon2015severity} or with standardized indices such as the SRI/SSI \cite{shukla2008sri,vicenteserrano2012ssi}, which are analogues of the SPI and SPEI \cite{mckee1993spi,vicenteserrano2010spei}. Parametric SSI values are sensitive to the chosen distribution \cite{tijdeman2020ssi}, which motivates nonparametric plotting-position formulations \cite{farahmand2015nonparametric,gringorten1963}. Drought forecasting requires an explicit identification step after the streamflow forecast, and the chosen definition changes the result \cite{sutanto2021definitions,hao2018prediction,sucozhanay2026threshold}. We therefore score drought days explicitly instead of inferring them from RMSE.

\section{Study Area and Data}
\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Discharge.} We use quality-controlled daily discharge from the Andean-GC dataset \cite{aguayo2026andeangc}, released under CC~BY~4.0. We keep every Peruvian gauge (SENAMHI) and every Altiplano gauge of the Lauca/Caquena headwaters in Chile (DGA) with at least 5000 quality-controlled days, which gives \nGauges{} gauges (\nPeru{} Peru, \nChile{} Chile; Table~\ref{tab:gauges}, Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). The focus gauge is Puente Isla Cabanillas (\texttt{P00000055}; 2843~km$^2$, mean elevation 4471~m), which drains the Coata--Cabanillas system into Lake Titicaca. The Cordillera Blanca gauges are historical records from 1952--1974 in strongly glacierized catchments. The remaining gauges span 1950--2024. Records contain multi-year gaps (e.g., 1996--2005 at Cabanillas), which we keep as missing values on a daily calendar.

\textbf{Meteorological forcing.} Daily precipitation and 2-m temperature come from ERA5 \cite{hersbach2020era5} at each catchment centroid, retrieved through the Open-Meteo historical API \cite{openmeteo}. The API's ERA5-Land endpoint \cite{munozsabater2021era5land} does not provide precipitation, so we use ERA5 throughout. A second, independent precipitation estimate is NASA POWER \texttt{PRECTOTCORR} (MERRA-2 bias-corrected with GPCP; available from 1981).

\textbf{Simulated discharge.} GloFAS~v4 reanalysis discharge is retrieved from the Open-Meteo Flood API (available from 1984) on a $3\times3$ neighbourhood of 0.05$^\circ$ cells around each gauge. The cell used is the one with the highest KGE over the fitting period only, so the test period is never used for this choice.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\textbf{Forecast set-up.} For each gauge and lead $h\in\{1,3,7\}$ days, a forecast issued at day $t$ predicts $Q_{t+h}$ using information available at $t$. The \nFeatures{} predictors are: discharge lags $Q_{t-k}$, $k\in\{0,1,2,3,7,14,30\}$; the one-day change; rolling means and standard deviations over 7, 14 and 30 days ending at $t$; precipitation lags (0--7 days) and 7-, 30- and 90-day totals; 2-m temperature (daily, 7- and 30-day means); and the day of year encoded as sine and cosine. All operations use a daily calendar index, so a lag of $k$ days is exactly $k$ days. Any sample whose predictors or target fall in a data gap is dropped. Persistence uses the same $Q_t$ that the learners see.

\textbf{Methods compared.} There are two references: persistence and a day-of-year climatology (15-day smoothed mean of the fitting period, evaluated at the target date). We compare six learners: Ridge regression \cite{hoerl1970ridge}, random forest \cite{breiman2001rf}, histogram gradient boosting \cite{friedman2001gbm}, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, a two-layer multilayer perceptron, and a low-flow-oriented XGBoost trained on the change in $\log(1+Q)$ (XGBoost-log). For every learner, a small grid of hyper-parameters is searched together with the target form: the level $Q_{t+h}$ or the change $Q_{t+h}-Q_t$.

\textbf{Validation protocol.} Each record is split chronologically into 70\% training, 15\% validation and 15\% test. The last $h$ samples of the training and validation blocks are removed (embargo) so that no target overlaps the next block. Configurations are selected on validation RMSE. The selected configuration is refit on training plus validation and scored once on the test block. When we refer to the learner of a gauge, we mean the one with the lowest validation RMSE, so the test set is never used to choose models. As a robustness check, an expanding-window walk-forward with three folds is run for Ridge at every gauge, re-selecting hyper-parameters within each fold.

\textbf{Scores and uncertainty.} We report RMSE, NSE \cite{nash1970}, KGE \cite{klinggupta2009,knoben2019kge} and the RMSE skill against persistence, $S=1-\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{model}}/\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{pers}}$ \cite{murphy1988skill}. Confidence intervals (95\%) for $S$ come from a paired moving-block bootstrap (\nBoot{} replicates, \bootBlock-day blocks) that preserves autocorrelation \cite{kunsch1989block,politis1994stationary}. Equal squared-error accuracy against persistence is tested with the Diebold--Mariano test using the small-sample correction \cite{diebold1995dm,harvey1997dm}.

\textbf{Drought detection.} Following the variable-threshold level method \cite{vanloon2015explained,fleig2006threshold,sutanto2021definitions}, a day is in hydrological drought when $Q$ falls below the monthly 20th percentile (Q80 exceedance) of the fitting period. For each method, we compare the forecast drought state at $t+h$ with the observed state and compute the probability of detection (POD), false-alarm ratio (FAR) and critical success index (CSI) \cite{schaefer1990csi}. For description we also compute a nonparametric SSI-30: the 30-day mean discharge is ranked within each calendar month, converted to a Gringorten plotting position, and mapped to a standard-normal quantile \cite{farahmand2015nonparametric,gringorten1963}. This avoids the distributional sensitivity of parametric SSI \cite{tijdeman2020ssi}.

\textbf{Data-fusion ablation.} Where MERRA-2 precipitation and GloFAS overlap the record (1984 onwards), we refit Ridge and XGBoost on identical samples with two predictor sets: ERA5 only, and ERA5 plus MERRA-2 precipitation and lagged GloFAS discharge up to $t$. GloFAS values at the target date are never used as predictors. We also report the raw GloFAS simulation as a physically based reference.

\textbf{Spatial statistics.} Spatial drought coherence is quantified as the Spearman correlation of monthly SSI-30 between every gauge pair with at least 60 overlapping months, as a function of great-circle distance $d$. We fit the exponential correlogram $\rho(d)=c\,e^{-d/d_0}$, in the spirit of space--time drought analysis \cite{tallaksen2009spacetime}. Pairs share gauges and are therefore not independent, so the 95\% interval of the decorrelation distance $d_0$ comes from a gauge-level bootstrap (500 replicates). Spatial clustering of the 7-day skill is tested with global Moran's $I$ \cite{moran1950}, using inverse-distance weights and 9999 permutations. Candidate drivers of skill are Andean-GC catchment attributes (area, mean elevation, glacier fraction, aridity, precipitation concentration, snow fraction and dry-day frequency). They are screened with Spearman correlations, permutation $p$-values and a Holm correction for multiple testing. The skill map and the drought-synchrony network are rendered in QGIS.

The whole pipeline runs on a laptop CPU (10 cores, about \runtimeMin{}~min). Every number in this paper is written automatically from the result tables.
