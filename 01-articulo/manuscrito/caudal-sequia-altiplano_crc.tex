\abstract*{Machine-learning studies of Andean streamflow rarely compare their models with persistence, and their validation designs can leak information from the test period. We built an open benchmark of \nGauges{} long-record gauges from the Andean Glacierized Catchment dataset in three regions: the Altiplano, including Puente Isla Cabanillas in the Lake Titicaca basin, the Cordillera Blanca, and the western and southern Andes. \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rivers of the tropical Andes supply irrigation, hydropower and drinking water, but their flow is strongly seasonal and exposed to glacier retreat and drought \cite{zubieta2021titicaca}. On the Altiplano, rivers such as the Coata--Cabanillas feed Lake Titicaca and depend on a short austral-summer wet season \cite{garreaud2003altiplano}, and hydrological droughts are projected to become longer and more frequent \cite{zubieta2021titicaca}. Short-range forecasts and early drought signals are therefore useful, but gauges are sparse and records are gappy.

Machine learning (ML) is widely used to forecast streamflow \cite{nearing2021role}. At daily leads, however, discharge is so autocorrelated that persistence, $\hat{Q}_{t+h}=Q_t$, is hard to beat \cite{pappenberger2015benchmarks}. Reported gains can be inflated by weak references, by shuffled cross-validation that leaks temporal information \cite{bergmeir2012cv,torres2026blockedcv}, and by giving the baseline and the learners different information at the time of issue. An earlier version of our own pipeline made the last error: the learners saw discharge only up to $t-1$ while persistence used $Q_t$, and calendar gaps were treated as consecutive days. Correcting both reversed the one-day conclusion.

The aim of this study was to measure how much ML adds to persistence for 1- to 7-day streamflow and drought forecasts in the Andes when no information leaks from the test period. The paper makes four contributions: (i) an open benchmark of \nGauges{} gauges in three hydroclimatic regions; (ii) a leakage-free protocol checked by automated tests; (iii) skill against persistence with confidence intervals, compared with earlier Andean studies; and (iv) a direct evaluation of drought-day detection and of its spatial coherence. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Sect.~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and methods, Sect.~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them with their limitations, and Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Random forests and LSTM networks have been used for hourly runoff forecasting in Ecuadorian Andean catchments \cite{munoz2018rf,contreras2021rf,merizalde2023lstm}. In Chile, LSTM networks supported flood warning in the Maipo basin \cite{delafuente2019lstm}, and random forests predicted monthly snowmelt-driven flows in the Elqui basin \cite{nunez2023xai}. Support-vector regression was tested for monthly runoff in the Central Andes of Argentina \cite{teverovsky2023svr}. In Peru, neural networks forecast monthly flows in the Crisnejas basin \cite{ramos2023crisnejas}, and $k$-nearest neighbours forecast daily flows of the Ramis River on the Altiplano \cite{quispe2023ramis}. Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarizes these studies. Seven of the eight used a single target gauge, none compared the models with persistence, and none reported an uncertainty estimate for the skill. For Peruvian agricultural data, blocked cross-validation showed that random folds overstate accuracy \cite{torres2026blockedcv}. Hydrological drought is a below-normal availability of streamflow \cite{vanloon2015explained}, and its forecast needs an explicit identification step \cite{sutanto2021definitions}. We therefore score drought days directly.

\textbf{Data.} Figure~\ref{fig:flow} summarizes the workflow. We used quality-controlled (QC) daily discharge from the Andean-GC dataset \cite{aguayo2026andeangc}. We kept every Peruvian gauge of the National Meteorological and Hydrological Service (SENAMHI) and every Chilean Altiplano gauge of the General Directorate of Water (DGA) with at least 5000 QC days. This gave \nGauges{} gauges: \nGaugesAlti{} on the Altiplano, \nGaugesCB{} in the glacier-fed Cordillera Blanca, and \nGaugesWSA{} in the western and southern Andes (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). The focus gauge was Puente Isla Cabanillas (2843~km$^2$, mean elevation 4471~m), which drains into Lake Titicaca. Daily precipitation and 2-m temperature came from ERA5 \cite{hersbach2020era5} at each catchment centroid through Open-Meteo \cite{openmeteo}. MERRA-2 precipitation and GloFAS~v4 discharge were added in a data-fusion test.

\textbf{Forecasts and learners.} A forecast issued on day $t$ predicts $Q_{t+h}$, $h\in\{1,3,7\}$ days, from \nFeatures{} predictors available at $t$. These were discharge lags (0 to 30 days), 7- to 30-day rolling means and standard deviations, precipitation lags and 7- to 90-day totals, temperature means, and the day of year. A lag of $k$ days is exactly $k$ calendar days, and samples touching a gap were dropped. The references were persistence, using the same $Q_t$ as the learners, and a day-of-year climatology. The six learners were Ridge regression, random forest, histogram gradient boosting, XGBoost, a two-layer perceptron (MLP), and an XGBoost trained on the change in $\log(1+Q)$ (XGBoost-log).

\textbf{Protocol.} Each record was split chronologically into 70\% training, 15\% validation and 15\% test, with an $h$-day embargo between blocks. Hyper-parameters and the target form (level or change) were chosen on the validation root-mean-square error (RMSE). The chosen model was refit on training plus validation and scored once on test, so the test set never selected models. Ridge was also run in a three-fold expanding walk-forward at every gauge. Automated tests check calendar offsets, the absence of future information and the embargo.

\textbf{Scores.} We report the Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) and the skill $S=1-\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{model}}/\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{pers}}$. Its 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) came from a moving-block bootstrap \cite{kunsch1989block}, and equal accuracy was tested with the Diebold--Mariano (DM) test \cite{diebold1995dm}. A day was in drought when $Q$ fell below the monthly 20th percentile of the fitting period \cite{vanloon2015explained}. Detection was scored with the probability of detection (POD), false-alarm ratio (FAR) and critical success index (CSI) \cite{schaefer1990csi}. Drought synchrony was measured with the correlation of a nonparametric 30-day standardized streamflow index (SSI-30) \cite{farahmand2015nonparametric} between gauge pairs, fitted as $\rho(d)=c\,e^{-d/d_0}$ against distance $d$. Spatial clustering of skill was tested with Moran's $I$.

\textbf{All gauges.} The learner beat persistence at \nPosOne{}/\nGauges{}, \nPosThree{}/\nGauges{} and \nPosSeven{}/\nGauges{} gauges at 1, 3 and 7 days, and its CI excluded zero at \nSigOne{}, \nSigThree{} and \nSigSeven{} gauges (Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}). The median skill grew from \medSkillOne{} to \medSkillThree{} and \medSkillSeven{}, because persistence degraded faster than the learners. Between one and seven days it rose from \medSkillCBOne{} to \medSkillCBSeven{} in the Cordillera Blanca and from \medSkillWSAOne{} to \medSkillWSASeven{} in the western and southern Andes, but stayed flat on the Altiplano (\medSkillAltiOne{} to \medSkillAltiSeven{}). Ridge alone was skilful at \nRidgePosSeven{}/\nGauges{} gauges at seven days, and \wfNPos{} of \wfNFolds{} walk-forward folds had positive skill (median \wfMedSkill{}). Adding MERRA-2 and GloFAS changed the seven-day Ridge skill by a median of only \fusionMedDeltaRidge{}.

\textbf{Drought detection.} Lower RMSE did not give better detection of drought days (Fig.~\ref{fig:drought}). At seven days, the median CSI was \medCsiPers{} for persistence and \medCsiBest{} for the learner, which beat persistence at only \nCsiBestBeats{}/\nGauges{} gauges; XGBoost-log reached \medCsiXgbLog{}. The learners raised fewer false alarms (median FAR \medFarBest{} vs.\ \medFarPers{}) but missed more drought days (POD \medPodBest{} vs.\ \medPodPers{}).

\textbf{Spatial statistics.} The SSI-30 correlation of \nPairs{} gauge pairs decreased with distance (Spearman \rhoDist{}, $p$~\pDist{}), with $d_0=\corrDzero$~km (95\% CI \corrDzeroLo--\corrDzeroHi~km), and was higher within regions than across them (median \rhoSameRegion{} vs.\ \rhoCrossRegion{}). Skill showed no spatial clustering (Moran's $I=\moranI$, $p=\moranP$). It decreased with snow fraction ($\rho=\topDriverRho$, Holm-adjusted $p=\topDriverPholm$), precipitation concentration and aridity, which describe the Altiplano headwaters.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The largest change in our conclusions came from the protocol, not from the model. When persistence and the learners received the same information and lags followed the calendar, a one-day loss at Cabanillas became a significant gain. The gains are modest (median RMSE reduction of 5--10\%) but consistent across gauges and folds, and linear models were as good as tree ensembles, as in large-sample studies \cite{nearing2021role}.

This helps to read the earlier Andean studies (Table~\ref{tab:lit}). Their scores are high, often NSE above 0.9 at short leads, but none was compared with persistence. At Cabanillas, persistence alone reached NSE~$=$~\focusPersNseOne{} at one day and the best learner \focusBestNseOne{}. For the Ramis River, a $k$-NN model with only the previous day's discharge reached NSE~$=$~0.965, against 0.979 for the best model \cite{quispe2023ramis}. In both rivers most of the NSE comes from persistence, so a high NSE is not evidence of skill at daily leads.

The Altiplano was the hardest region: its short convective pulses and long recessions are only partly resolved by a 0.25$^\circ$ reanalysis \cite{garreaud2003altiplano,lujano2025gridded}. Drought detection did not follow RMSE because models trained on squared error smooth the recession limb near the threshold, so drought warnings should be trained and verified on the drought decision itself \cite{sutanto2021definitions}.

\textbf{Limitations.} ERA5 is coarse for steep catchments. The Cordillera Blanca records predate MERRA-2 and GloFAS, so the fusion test covered \nFusion{} gauges only. One validation block can be unstable under non-stationarity, as the seven-day selection at Cabanillas showed. Predictors used observed forcing, not weather forecasts, and Bolivian records were not available.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We released an open, leakage-free benchmark for 1- to 7-day streamflow and drought forecasting at \nGauges{} Andean gauges. The learners beat persistence at \nPosSeven{}/\nGauges{} gauges at seven days (median skill \medSkillSeven{}) and at one day at Cabanillas, but the gains did not carry over to drought-day detection. Future work will add drought-aware losses, operational weather forecasts and more Altiplano gauges.
